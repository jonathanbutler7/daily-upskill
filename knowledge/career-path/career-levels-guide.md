Introduction
At Weave, software engineering encompasses a broad spectrum of tasks and responsibilities, varying significantly in scope and complexity. The department is committed to delivering high-quality software to both internal and external customers. To achieve this, we place a strong emphasis on our senior engineers and team leads. Their extensive experience is crucial in navigating the difficult balance between rapid and secure delivery, while simultaneously upholding product quality. This document details the diverse roles and responsibilities of software engineers at Weave. Demonstrating Weave's values, effective communication, integrity in commitments, and professionalism are essential at all levels.


Career Frameworks at Weave
Growth Track vs Career Track
At Weave, we recognize two distinct career tracks that apply to both Individual Contributors and Management roles: Growth Track and Career Track. Understanding the distinction between these tracks is essential for setting appropriate expectations for career progression.

Growth Track: These levels represent the stages of a career where individuals are developing their skills and working toward professional mastery. People in Growth roles are generally expected to advance, and a lack of progression may lead to performance concerns. Growth Track includes:

Individual Contributors: P1 (Jr Engineer), P2-I (Engineer), P2-II (Engineer II)
Management: M2 (Team Lead)

Career Track: Career roles offer a long-term career path with significant responsibility and autonomy, where continued progression is not a requirement for successful performance. These roles represent professional mastery and are sustainable terminal levels where people can have highly successful careers without advancing further. Career Track includes:

Individual Contributors: P3-I (Senior Engineer), P3-II (Senior Engineer II), P4-I (Staff Engineer), P4-II (Staff Engineer II), P5 (Principal Engineer)
Management: M3 (Engineering Manager), M4 (Senior Engineering Manager), M5 (Engineering Director), M6 (Senior Engineering Director)

Why This Matters: The distinction creates clear expectations. In Growth Track roles, advancement is part of normal development. In Career Track roles, advancement becomes increasingly selective and requires both exceptional performance AND organizational need. P3-I (Senior Engineer) represents the first Career Track level for Individual Contributors—engineers who reach P3-I have achieved professional mastery and can build highly successful, long-term careers at this level.
Promotion Philosophy
Promotions recognize sustained performance already demonstrated at the next level. A promotion confirms work that has been happening, a change that has already happened; it is not a bet that the person will grow into the role once the title is granted. Sufficient experience is a necessary input, but time alone never qualifies someone for promotion.

Every promotion is governed by three requirements:

Time in level. Generally at least one year at the current level. This is distinct from the total years-of-experience guidance in each role's competency table; years in the field and time in the current level are different measures.

Sustained performance at the next level. Candidates typically accumulate the two cycles of next-level evidence through stretch assignments that carry next-level scope. Granting that scope to high-potential engineers ahead of promotion is a normal part of how we develop people, and performance during a stretch assignment counts toward the observation window.
 
Organizational need. For Career Track promotions, the organization must also need additional capacity at that level; strong performance alone does not create a position.


Individual Contributor Career Path
Individual Contributors (ICs) create impact through their technical work and influence rather than through managing people. At Weave, ICs progress through eight levels from P1 (Jr Engineer) to P5 (Principal Engineer), with scope and influence expanding at each level.
How Scope and Autonomy Evolve
As engineers progress through IC levels, their ownership scope expands from contributing to features (P1 - P2-I) to owning feature areas (P2-II) to owning systems (P3-I) to owning multiple systems and enabling team success (P3-II) to owning platforms across teams (P4-I - P4-II) to shaping technical strategy for entire domains (P5). Similarly, their autonomy grows from executing well-defined tasks with guidance to proactively identifying and solving company-wide technical challenges.

Problem Definition Evolution:

P1: Given both what to build AND how to build it
P2-I: Given what to build, figures out how
P2-II: Given outcomes, identifies problems and solutions with light guidance
P3-I: Given outcomes, identifies problems to solve and how to solve them
P3-II: Proactively identifies problems; defines technical approach for team
P4-I: Proactively identifies cross-team problems, defines strategic approaches
P4-II: Identifies department-level problems; shapes technical agenda
P5: Identifies company-level technical problems/opportunities, shapes strategic direction

Ownership Evolution:

P1: Contributes to systems others own
P2-I: Owns components within systems
P2-II: Owns features end-to-end within established systems
P3-I: Owns one or more systems end-to-end (first level of full system ownership)
P3-II: Owns multiple interconnected systems; shapes team's technical direction
P4-I: Owns platforms/multiple interconnected systems across teams within fellowship
P4-II: Owns technical strategy for major domain spanning fellowships
P5: Owns technical strategy and direction for major domains spanning departments
How Daily Work Evolves
As engineers advance, the nature of their daily work shifts significantly in terms of time allocation:

P1: Primarily hands-on coding and learning (80-90% of time on implementation)
P2-I: Hands-on coding with growing independence (75-85% on implementation)
P2-II: Hands-on coding plus mentoring and cross-team coordination (70-80% on implementation)
P3-I: Balanced technical work including coding, reviews, architecture, and mentorship (60-75% hands-on technical work)
P3-II: Balanced work with increased focus on enabling others (55-70% hands-on technical work)
P4-I: Strategic technical work with selective hands-on contributions (30-40% hands-on technical work including coding, architectural prototyping, and technical reviews)
P4-II: Strategic leadership with targeted technical contributions (25-35% hands-on technical work)
P5: Strategic leadership and organizational influence (20-30% hands-on technical work, with remainder focused on strategy, cross-department coordination, and organizational impact)

The percentage of time spent writing code decreases with seniority not because technical skills matter less, but because impact increasingly comes from enabling others, making strategic decisions, and solving problems at organizational scale.

Management Career Path
Management roles represent a distinct career path from Individual Contributor roles, with different expectations, skills, and measures of success. While ICs create impact through their technical work and influence, managers create impact by building high-performing teams and enabling others to deliver exceptional results.
Team Lead (M2) - The Developmental Entry Point
The Team Lead role serves as the entry point into people management, bridging the gap between individual contribution and full people leadership. Team Leads balance significant hands-on technical work (approximately 80%) with foundational people leadership responsibilities (approximately 20%), making this an ideal developmental role for engineers exploring the management track.

Spending time as a Team Lead is the expected and strongly preferred path toward Engineering Manager, providing essential experience in team leadership, performance management, and balancing technical work with people responsibilities before taking on the full scope of an Engineering Manager role.
Management Career Track (M3-M6)
Beginning at Engineering Manager (M3), management becomes a distinct Career Track where continued progression is not required for successful performance. M3 represents management mastery and is a sustainable terminal level where managers can have highly successful, long-term careers. Advancement from M3 to M4 and beyond is considerably more challenging, requiring both consistently demonstrated performance at the next level AND organizational need for expanded leadership scope.
How Scope and Span Evolve
As managers progress through levels, their span of control expands significantly:

M2 (Team Lead): 3-5 engineers, ~80% technical / ~20% people leadership
M3 (Engineering Manager): 5-9 engineers, ~50% technical / ~50% people leadership
M4 (Senior Engineering Manager): Multiple teams (via Team Leads/EMs or direct), strategic leadership focus
M5 (Engineering Director): Fellowship (3+ managers and teams), strategic planning and organizational leadership
M6 (Senior Engineering Director): Multiple fellowships or functions, company-wide impact

Their scope evolves from tactical execution and team-level outcomes (M2-M3) to strategic planning and organizational-wide impact (M4-M6). While M2-M3 focus primarily on their team's delivery and growth, M4-M6 increasingly focus on developing other leaders, establishing organizational practices, and shaping strategic direction.

The technical portion of an M2 or M3's duties is not limited only to coding, but should include writing and reviewing TPDs and other technical documentation, reviewing code, pairing, defining architecture, etc.

Choosing Between IC and Management Tracks
The decision between IC and management tracks is not about advancement or prestige—both tracks lead to senior, impactful roles. The key question is: Do you want your primary impact to come through your own technical work and influence, or through building and leading teams?

Individual Contributors create impact through:

Technical expertise and deep problem-solving
Architectural decisions and technical strategy
Technical leadership across teams and domains
Mentoring engineers and elevating technical capabilities
Staying hands-on with systems and code

Managers create impact through:

Building and developing high-performing teams
Creating environments where engineers thrive
Strategic planning and organizational coordination
People development and career growth
Balancing technical needs with business objectives

Both paths require leadership, but the nature of that leadership differs fundamentally. Engineers should explore Team Lead roles to experience people leadership before committing to the management track, and the company supports transitions between tracks at appropriate levels when it makes sense for both the individual and the organization.


Individual Contributor Roles
[P1] Jr Engineer, Growth
Writes production quality code with direction from the team, focus on growth and independence
Role Overview
Jr. Engineers are new to the field of software engineering, embarking on a period of intense learning where theoretical knowledge gained from college or certificate programs is applied in a real-world setting. They work on well-defined tasks within systems owned by more senior engineers, typically focusing on work outside the critical path with direct mentorship. As members of the Growth Track, Jr. Engineers are expected to develop toward P2-I, with progression being a normal expectation rather than an exceptional achievement.
Technical Scope & Ownership
Jr. Engineers contribute to existing systems and features with guidance from their team. They work on smaller, well-defined tasks where both what needs to be built and how to build it are clearly specified by more senior engineers. They are accountable for their code and learn to take ownership of their mistakes, treating each as a learning opportunity. Their focus is on writing clean, well-tested code using established patterns and tools (such as Git, testing frameworks, and team coding standards) rather than defining new approaches.
Autonomy & Problem-Solving
Jr. Engineers are given well-defined problems with clear specifications for both the objective (what) and the implementation approach (how). They work with close mentorship, asking questions to clarify requirements and seeking help proactively when blocked. Their primary responsibility is executing assigned tasks correctly and on time, learning to break down small tasks into manageable steps. They develop the judgment to know when to seek help—neither struggling too long independently nor asking prematurely before attempting to solve problems themselves.
Collaboration & Quality
Jr. Engineers focus on team collaboration, learning team processes, and communicating effectively within their immediate team. They demonstrate professionalism through strong written and verbal communication, the ability to give and receive feedback constructively, and commitment to teamwork. They are accountable for maintaining code quality in their work through unit testing, following code review feedback, and adhering to team standards. While they are still learning to assess code quality, they develop an understanding of what high-quality code looks like through the code review process and mentorship from senior team members.
AI Tooling & Automation
Jr. Engineers develop foundational proficiency with AI-assisted development tools as part of their technical growth. They use AI coding assistants (such as GitHub Copilot, Cursor, or Claude) with guidance from senior engineers, learning to critically evaluate AI-generated suggestions rather than accepting them uncritically. They follow established team practices for AI tool usage, including when AI assistance is appropriate and when traditional approaches are preferred. Jr. Engineers learn to recognize the limitations of AI tools—understanding that AI suggestions require the same scrutiny as any other code and may contain subtle errors, security issues, or architectural misalignments. As they develop this judgment, they increasingly use AI tools to accelerate learning, explore unfamiliar codebases, and improve their productivity on well-defined tasks.
Success Measures
Success for Jr. Engineers is measured by their ability to deliver production-ready code on time, their growth velocity in developing technical and professional skills, and their effectiveness as collaborative team members. Key indicators include completing well-defined tasks independently with decreasing need for guidance, understanding and following team processes and priorities, and demonstrating continuous learning. Jr. Engineers who consistently seek feedback, learn from mistakes, and show steady progress toward P2-I-level autonomy are on track for successful advancement to the next level.
Role Boundaries
Jr. Engineers are not:
Students or interns still in training - They write production code they are accountable for. Learning happens through owning real work and its outcomes, not in place of delivery.
P2-Is who need closer supervision - The line is who defines the "how." Jr. Engineers are given both the problem and the implementation approach; being handed the problem and choosing the approach within established patterns is the P2-I move.
Exempt from accountability because they're learning - They own their code and their mistakes. Mentorship supports that ownership; it doesn't replace it.

[P2-I] Engineer I, Growth
Code Craftsman. Delivers code quickly and independently
Role Overview
Engineer I represents the first stage of mid-level engineering, where engineers have moved beyond the learning-intensive junior phase and demonstrate proficiency in core tools and languages. They distinguish themselves from Jr. Engineers through their ability to independently execute routine work without constant guidance. As members of the Growth Track, P2-I Engineers are expected to continue developing toward P2-II, with this progression being a normal part of career development. Engineer I professionals leverage established patterns to develop high-quality, well-tested, and maintainable solutions, taking growing ownership of their work. Engineers at this level typically have 2-3 years of experience.
Technical Scope & Ownership
Engineer I professionals own specific components within systems, taking responsibility for the quality, maintenance, and evolution of their areas. They are given problems to solve (what) and determine the appropriate implementation approach (how), choosing from established patterns and architectures. They understand how their components fit into larger systems and can independently fix bugs, add enhancements, and refactor their code. While they work within systems designed by more senior engineers, they take ownership of their components' success and can identify edge cases and potential issues in requirements.
Autonomy & Problem-Solving
Engineer I professionals are given problems to solve and independently determine implementation approaches, working within established architectural patterns. They identify edge cases, clarify ambiguous requirements, and propose solutions when requirements are incomplete. They require minimal day-to-day oversight on routine work, though they seek guidance on complex technical decisions or when navigating organizational complexity. They proactively communicate project status, identify potential blockers early, and seek feedback on their approaches before investing significant time in implementation.
Collaboration, Mentorship & Quality
Engineer I professionals collaborate effectively with team members and support cross-fellowship work in a collaborative capacity. They begin to support Jr. Engineers through pair programming and answering questions. They are accountable for maintaining high code quality standards through comprehensive testing, thoughtful code reviews, and adherence to team practices. They ensure their own code meets quality standards and help identify technical debt and defects in their areas of ownership.
AI Tooling & Automation
Engineer I professionals begin operating as independent users of AI-assisted development tools. They leverage AI coding assistants and other aids to accelerate routine work, focusing on crafting effective prompts and providing appropriate context to achieve useful results. They are expected to critically evaluate all AI-generated code for correctness, security, maintainability, and alignment with established team patterns before integration, treating AI outputs as raw materials requiring professional scrutiny. They contribute to discussions on AI tool effectiveness within their team.
Success Measures
Success for Engineer I is measured by their ability to independently deliver high-quality, well-tested components on time with minimal oversight. Key indicators include ownership of component areas with few production issues, effective collaboration and support of team initiatives, and demonstrated growth toward P2-II-level feature ownership and autonomy. Engineer I professionals who consistently deliver quality code, understand stakeholder needs, and show readiness to own complete features are on track for advancement.
Role Boundaries
P2-I professionals are not:
Jr. Engineers who work faster - The shift is problem framing: given the problem and choosing the implementation approach within established patterns, rather than given both. They own components, not just contribute to systems others own.
P2-Is who haven't been promoted yet - P2-II is a conjunction: complete features end-to-end and active P1 mentorship they're sought out for and a smaller cross-team effort they led rather than supported. P2-I owns components, seeks guidance on ambiguous requirements, helps P1s when asked, and plays a supporting role cross-team. When the evidence is mixed, the placement is P2-I with a development plan.
Feature owners - They own components within systems more senior engineers designed. Owning a feature from design through delivery begins at P2-II.

[P2-II] Engineer II, Growth
Feature Owner. Delivers complete features independently and mentors junior engineers
Role Overview
Engineer II represents the advanced stage of mid-level engineering, demonstrating strong capability in independent feature execution and beginning to influence beyond their immediate work. They distinguish themselves from Engineer I through their end-to-end feature ownership and active mentorship of junior engineers. As members of the Growth Track, P2-II Engineers are expected to continue developing toward P3-I (Senior Engineer I). This progression represents an important inflection point: P3-I is the first Career Track level, where engineers achieve professional mastery with full system ownership and can build long-term, successful careers without needing to advance further. Engineer II professionals operate with significant independence, taking comprehensive ownership of their features from design through delivery.
Technical Scope & Ownership
Engineer II professionals own features end-to-end within established systems, taking full responsibility for the quality, delivery, and evolution of their feature areas. They are given outcomes to achieve and identify problems and solutions with light guidance from senior engineers. They understand system dependencies and integration points, making informed decisions about implementation approaches. They actively manage technical debt within their feature areas and can propose improvements to system design within their domain.
Autonomy & Problem-Solving
Engineer II professionals operate independently on most work, translating desired outcomes into technical approaches with minimal oversight. They seek guidance only on complex architectural decisions or novel technical challenges. They proactively identify edge cases, clarify ambiguous requirements, and propose solutions that consider broader system implications. They communicate project status effectively, anticipate blockers across sprint boundaries, and coordinate with adjacent teams when their work has cross-team implications.
Collaboration, Mentorship & Quality
Engineer II professionals collaborate effectively across teams and actively mentor P1 engineers through pair programming, code reviews, and knowledge sharing. They lead smaller cross-team efforts and contribute meaningfully to fellowship-wide initiatives. They are accountable for maintaining high code quality standards, serving as trusted reviewers for their feature areas. They propose improvements to team standards and practices, contributing to continuous improvement of engineering processes.
AI Tooling & Automation
Engineer II professionals are proficient, independent users of AI-assisted development tools. They effectively leverage AI coding assistants, automated testing tools, and other AI-powered development aids to accelerate their work while maintaining code quality. They understand how to craft effective prompts and provide appropriate context to get useful results from AI tools—recognizing that the quality of AI output depends significantly on how requests are framed. They exercise sound judgment about when AI assistance adds value versus when traditional approaches are more appropriate—particularly in security-sensitive code, complex algorithmic work, or areas requiring deep domain knowledge. They critically evaluate AI-generated code for correctness, maintainability, and alignment with team patterns before incorporating it into their work. They contribute to team discussions about AI tool effectiveness and may suggest improvements to team AI practices based on their experience.
Success Measures
Success for Engineer II is measured by their ability to independently deliver complete features on time with high quality, their effectiveness as mentors, and their growing cross-team influence. Key indicators include ownership of feature areas with few production issues, effective mentorship of junior engineers, meaningful contributions to cross-team initiatives, and demonstrated readiness to own complete systems. Engineer II professionals who consistently deliver well-architected features, elevate junior engineers, and show system-level thinking are on track for advancement to Senior Engineer I.
Role Boundaries
P2-II professionals are not:
P2-Is with more tenure - All three must hold: end-to-end feature ownership, P1 mentorship they're sought out for, and a smaller cross-team effort they led. Any one alone doesn't make the level, and it's judged on a sustained pattern across review cycles, not a standout project. P3-I adds full system ownership, including operational responsibility such as on-call and incident response. P2-II owns features within systems that others own and operate. "Ready for full system ownership" is the hinge - "not yet" is P2-II, "yes or nearly" signals the P3-I transition.
Accountable for the team's code quality - They're trusted reviewers for their own feature areas and mentor P1s. Accountability for maintaining code-quality standards across the team begins at P3-I.

[P3-I] Senior Engineer I, Career
Seasoned Engineer. Technical leader on their engineering team, driving toward key outcomes with full system ownership
Role Overview
P3-I is the first Career Track level for Individual Contributors, representing professional mastery and full system ownership. Engineers who reach P3-I have "arrived"—they can have highly successful, long-term careers at this level without requiring advancement to P3-II or beyond. Senior Engineer I professionals work cross-functionally, demonstrating strong communication skills and the ability to present complex ideas to diverse audiences. Their experience allows them to balance tactical execution with strategic thinking about long-term system health.
Technical Scope & Ownership
Senior Engineer I professionals own one or more systems end-to-end, with complete accountability for system health, reliability, performance, and evolution. This includes operational responsibility such as on-call duties, incident response, and ongoing maintenance. They understand system dependencies and integration points, making architectural decisions for their systems while following established patterns and architectural designs. They bear significant responsibility for maintaining products and services in both the short and long term, actively managing technical debt within their domain.
Autonomy & Problem-Solving
Senior Engineer I professionals are given desired outcomes and independently identify the problems to solve and determine how to solve them. They excel in ambiguous situations, resolving uncertainty through planning, derisking, and seeking consensus. They focus on desired outcomes rather than specific outputs, maintaining broad awareness of business needs, customers, and company priorities. They lead the creation of Technical Project Documents (TPDs) for team-level projects, translating vague objectives into well-defined technical plans. They make decisions that balance immediate needs with long-term system sustainability.
Collaboration, Mentorship & Influence
Senior Engineer I professionals collaborate regularly within their team and coordinate with adjacent teams on shared dependencies. They actively mentor junior engineers (P1-P2-II) through code reviews, knowledge sharing, and technical guidance. They are accountable for ensuring code quality standards are maintained, serving as technical leaders who uphold quality expectations through thorough code reviews. They work directly with Product Managers to understand requirements, provide technical input, and ensure alignment between product vision and technical implementation.
Product Partnership & Documentation
Senior Engineer I professionals maintain open communication with Product Managers, collaborating on project planning and execution. They lead the creation of Technical Project Documents (TPDs) for team-level projects, ensuring technical decisions are well-documented and understood by stakeholders. They provide technical perspective during product planning, helping PMs understand feasibility, tradeoffs, and technical constraints.
Quality & Technical Excellence
Senior Engineer I professionals maintain high code quality through rigorous testing, thoughtful architecture, and comprehensive code reviews. They actively manage technical debt, balancing feature delivery with system sustainability. Their code is maintainable, scalable, and performant, serving as examples for more junior engineers. Approximately 60-75% of their time is spent on hands-on technical work including coding, architecture, and code reviews, with the remainder focused on mentorship, planning, and collaboration.
AI Tooling & Automation
Senior Engineer I professionals demonstrate advanced proficiency with AI-assisted development tools and begin to elevate their team’s AI-augmented capabilities. They master the effective use of AI tools across the development lifecycle, including code generation, review, and documentation. They mentor junior engineers (P1-P2-II) on effective AI tool usage, helping them develop the necessary judgment to use these tools productively. They actively evaluate and recommend new AI-powered techniques that could benefit their team and contribute to refining team practices for AI tool usage, ensuring high standards for code quality and security are maintained.
Success Measures
Success for Senior Engineer I is measured by their ability to independently own and evolve systems, deliver complete projects on time with high quality, and contribute meaningfully to team-level initiatives. Key indicators include system reliability and performance, successful delivery of customer-focused outcomes, effective collaboration, meaningful mentorship of junior engineers, and sound technical decision-making. Senior Engineer I professionals who consistently demonstrate cross-team influence and team enablement are on track for advancement to Senior Engineer II.
Role Boundaries
P3-I professionals are not:
P2-IIs promoted on tenure - P3-I is the first level with full system ownership: end-to-end accountability for a system's reliability, performance, and evolution, including operational duties. That operational ownership is the concrete line, not years served.
P2-IIs who haven't been promoted yet - P3-I applies the team's established patterns and leads their own projects from ambiguous goal to shipped. P3-II is generative, not reactive. Placement at P3-II requires evidence of at least two of the following from the last 6-12 months: a TPD or technical proposal they initiated that the team adopted, a cross-team coordination effort they led, or a P3-I or P2-II engineer who measurably improved under their direct involvement. Absent that evidence, the placement is P3-I.
Staff Engineers in waiting - Their scope is the systems they own and their team. Fellowship-level force multiplication across teams is the P4 shift, not a larger version of P3-I.

[P3-II] Senior Engineer II, Career
Force Multiplier. Technical leader who elevates team capability and shapes technical direction
Role Overview
Senior Engineer II represents the advanced stage of senior engineering, building on the professional mastery established at P3-I. Engineers at P3-II distinguish themselves through their focus on making others successful and shaping their team's technical direction — they not only own systems but actively elevate the capabilities of engineers around them. They regularly demonstrate behaviors expected at the Staff level, positioning them for future advancement if organizational need exists.
Technical Scope & Ownership
Senior Engineer II professionals own multiple interconnected systems, with accountability for how these systems work together cohesively. Beyond individual system ownership, they shape their team's overall technical direction, defining patterns and approaches that other engineers follow. They make architectural decisions that affect the entire team and anticipate technical challenges 2-3 quarters ahead. They serve as the team's technical conscience, ensuring decisions made today support long-term objectives.
Autonomy & Problem-Solving
Senior Engineer II professionals proactively identify problems before they're assigned, defining both what needs to be solved and the approach. They set architectural patterns for their team and are consulted on fellowship-level decisions. They excel at navigating the most ambiguous situations, charting paths forward when requirements and solutions are both unclear. They lead TPDs for cross-team projects and provide substantive review of others' TPDs, elevating the quality of technical planning across the team.
Collaboration, Mentorship & Influence
Senior Engineer II professionals are key connectors across the fellowship, leading cross-team technical initiatives and facilitating communication between teams. They actively mentor P1-P3-I engineers, with particular focus on developing future senior engineers. Their mentorship creates lasting impact—engineers they work with demonstrably improve. They are accountable for team technical quality, ensuring engineering excellence across their domain. They influence fellowship practices through their team's successes and by advocating for improvements.
Product Partnership & Documentation
Senior Engineer II professionals maintain strategic relationships with Product Managers, providing critical technical perspective on multi-team implications. They lead TPDs for cross-team projects, ensuring technical decisions are well-documented and aligned across teams. They help Product Managers understand complex technical tradeoffs and constraints, enabling better product decisions at the fellowship level.
Quality & Technical Excellence
Senior Engineer II professionals set the standard for technical excellence on their team. They drive standards and best practices, establishing patterns that become team norms. Their technical judgment is widely trusted, and they are sought out for guidance on challenging technical problems. Approximately 55-70% of their time is spent on hands-on technical work, with the remainder focused on cross-team coordination, mentorship, and enabling others' success.
AI Tooling & Automation
Senior Engineer II professionals demonstrate advanced proficiency with AI-assisted development tools and actively elevate their team's AI-augmented capabilities. They master the effective use of AI tools across the development lifecycle—from code generation and review to testing, documentation, and debugging. They stay current with rapidly evolving AI development capabilities, tracking new tools, features, and techniques that could benefit their team. They mentor junior engineers (P1-P3-I) on effective AI tool usage, helping them develop the judgment to use these tools productively while avoiding common pitfalls. Senior Engineers evaluate new AI development tools for potential team adoption, assessing their fit with existing workflows, security implications, and impact on code quality. They contribute to and refine team practices for AI tool usage, ensuring these practices evolve as tools mature. They recognize when AI-generated solutions require significant human refinement versus when they can be adopted with minimal modification.
Success Measures
Success for Senior Engineer II is measured by their impact as force multipliers on their team. Key indicators include:

Team Enablement: Engineers they mentor demonstrably improve; the team becomes more capable because of their presence
Technical Quality: Measurable improvements in team-wide technical quality and velocity
Cross-Team Influence: Successful delivery of cross-team initiatives; effective resolution of cross-team technical challenges
Pattern Adoption: Technical patterns and standards they establish are adopted across the team
Strategic Positioning: Technical contributions that position the team for future success

Senior Engineer II professionals who regularly demonstrate Staff-level behaviors (see Appendix: Staff+ Archetypes) are positioned for advancement when organizational need exists.
Role Boundaries
P3-II professionals are not:
P3-Is who take on harder problems - The distinction is generative versus reactive impact: they surface problems the team didn't know it had, set patterns others follow, and their technical framing shows up in work they didn't directly own. It's confirmed by the two-of-three evidence anchors - an adopted TPD they initiated, a cross-team effort they led, an engineer who improved under them - not by the difficulty of what they personally ship.
P4-I without the title - P3-II operates within their team. P4 is fellowship scope: owning platforms across teams and setting patterns other teams adopt. Regularly demonstrating Staff-level behavior positions a P3-II for advancement, but promotion to P4 also requires organizational need.
Defined by mentorship volume - Actively developing engineers is necessary but not sufficient. The distinguishing mark is that their technical framing and standards are visibly reflected in work they didn't own.

[P4-I] Staff Engineer, Career
Cross-Team Technical Leader. Force multiplier who drives impact across the fellowship and influences technical direction
Role Overview
Staff Engineers are highly trusted technical experts who solve complex, large-scale problems across team boundaries. Advancement to P4-I is considerably more challenging than earlier progressions and requires both consistently demonstrating Staff-level impact while at P3-II AND an organizational need for this level of technical leadership. Representing approximately 5% of engineers, Staff Engineers are force multipliers who make everyone around them more productive through technical leadership, strategic problem-solving, and organizational enablement. They act as "T-shaped" experts with deep understanding in key areas while regularly developing expertise in others, focusing on big-picture, long-term initiatives that span multiple teams.
Operating Modes (Archetypes)
Staff Engineers may emphasize different modes of operation depending on organizational need. All modes are valid paths to Staff-level impact:

Tech Lead Mode: Guides approach and execution for a cluster of 2-4 teams. Coordinates team execution, maintains cross-team relationships, and carries institutional context. Partners closely with Engineering Managers on team-level initiatives. Most common entry point to Staff engineering.

Architect Mode: Owns direction, quality, and approach within a critical technical domain such as API design, data platform, infrastructure, or frontend architecture. Maintains intimate understanding of business needs, user goals, and technical constraints simultaneously. Stretches into multi-year time horizons for architectural decisions.

Solver Mode: Tackles the hardest cross-cutting technical problems identified as critical by leadership. Works with a transient orientation, moving between problem areas as organizational needs shift. Requires careful handoffs to avoid frustrating teams left maintaining "solved" systems.

Most Staff Engineers blend elements of multiple modes, with emphasis shifting based on current organizational priorities. Mode is not a sub-level distinction. A Staff Engineer operating primarily in Tech Lead mode is not placed below one operating primarily in Architect mode.
Accountability & Decision Rights
Staff Engineers are Responsible for technical direction within their domain, while Engineering Managers and Directors are Accountable for delivery and organizational outcomes. This split is what separates the IC track from the management track at this level, not technical capability.

Staff Engineers are Responsible for: technical architecture decisions, technology selection within their domain, engineering standards and patterns, technical vision for their fellowship, and resolution of complex technical problems.

Engineering Managers and Directors are Accountable for: resource allocation, headcount decisions, team composition, performance management, career development conversations, team health, and delivery commitments.

When technical decisions require headcount or timeline tradeoffs, Staff Engineers provide technical direction while Engineering Managers make resourcing decisions. Both participate in the conversation; accountability determines who owns the final call.
Technical Scope & Ownership
Staff Engineers own platforms or multiple interconnected systems across teams within their fellowship, with accountability for how these systems work together cohesively. They lead the architectural design process across teams, defining patterns and standards that other engineers follow. They own the technical strategy for their domain, ensuring architectural coherence and making decisions that affect multiple teams. While they may not operate individual systems day-to-day, they shape system evolution and ensure technical decisions across teams align with long-term fellowship objectives. They discover and address significant fellowship-level technical challenges, creating reusable solutions that accelerate development across multiple teams.

Staff Engineers typically dedicate approximately 30-40% of their time to hands-on technical work including code reviews, architectural prototyping, technical spikes, and selective coding contributions. The remainder focuses on cross-team coordination, mentorship, technical strategy, and organizational enablement. The reduction from Senior Engineer II's 55-70% hands-on time reflects their expanded scope and the higher leverage of strategic work at this level.
Autonomy & Problem-Solving
Staff Engineers proactively identify important cross-team problems that others miss, operating with high autonomy to define both what problems to solve and the strategic approaches for solving them. They excel at navigating organizational complexity, working across team boundaries to align stakeholders and build consensus. They apply their expertise to the hardest technical challenges, regularly solving ambiguous problems where goals are vague and paths are unclear. They lead the creation of Technical Project Documents (TPDs) for major initiatives, setting technical direction and ensuring clear communication of architectural decisions across fellowships.

Staff Engineers operate with a strategic planning horizon of 2-4 quarters ahead, anticipating technical challenges and positioning their fellowship for long-term success. They balance quarterly execution with multi-year architectural thinking, ensuring decisions made today support the company's future needs.
Partnership Model with Management
Staff Engineers and Engineering Managers form complementary partnerships with distinct accountabilities:

The Staff Engineer partners with EMs on how systems get built, providing technical guidance, architectural support, and pushback on technical debt.
The Engineering Manager owns what to build in conjunction with PM and Design, and is ultimately accountable for making sure the right thing gets built in the right way.

Neither role replaces the other. Staff Engineers should not take over the technical aspects of an EM's role or act as a translation layer between technology and engineers—EMs remain technical participants in architectural discussions. The partnership works best when both roles respect each other's accountability domain while collaborating closely on shared goals. The main differentiator for the Staff level is scope and influence, specifically leading bigger projects across the fellowship with higher stakes.

Staff Engineers often work closely with multiple Engineering Managers and Product Managers, serving as technical thought partners on fellowship-level initiatives.
Collaboration, Mentorship & Influence
Staff Engineers lead cross-fellowship collaboration, coordinating work and removing roadblocks that affect multiple teams. They actively mentor engineers across the fellowship (P1-P3-II), elevating technical capabilities through code reviews, architectural guidance, and knowledge sharing. Their mentorship creates lasting impact as mentees develop into stronger engineers. They are accountable for maintaining engineering excellence across multiple teams, driving standards for the fellowship and ensuring technical quality at scale. They facilitate communication between teams, making other engineers more effective by "paving the path" and removing obstacles.

Developing P3-II engineers toward Staff readiness is a specific expectation at this level. The bench from which future Staff Engineers are drawn is built here.
Product Partnership & Documentation
Staff Engineers maintain strategic relationships with Product leadership, providing critical technical perspective on multi-team initiatives. They lead or significantly contribute to Technical Project Documents for major fellowship-level projects, ensuring technical feasibility, identifying risks, and defining implementation strategies. They help Product Managers understand complex technical tradeoffs and constraints, enabling better product decisions. They translate product vision into technical strategies that span multiple teams, ensuring cohesive execution.
Technical Leadership & Standards
Staff Engineers drive technical standards and best practices across the fellowship, establishing patterns that other teams adopt. They lead by influence without direct reports, using technical expertise and communication skills to shape how engineering is done across their domain. They handle technical escalations, provide architectural reviews for major projects, and create frameworks that accelerate product development. Their technical judgment is widely trusted, and they are sought out for guidance on the most challenging technical problems across multiple teams.
AI Tooling & Automation
Staff Engineers drive strategic AI tool adoption and development practices across their fellowship. They evaluate, select, and champion AI development tools that improve engineering velocity, code quality, and developer experience across multiple teams. Beyond using existing tools effectively, Staff Engineers build and customize AI-powered workflows tailored to Weave's specific codebase, patterns, and needs. This may include configuring AI assistants with company-specific context, creating custom automation pipelines, or developing internal tooling that uses AI capabilities. They establish standards and best practices for AI-assisted development that balance productivity gains with code quality, security, and maintainability. Staff Engineers measure the impact of AI tools on team velocity and quality metrics, using data to guide adoption decisions and identify areas for improvement. They help teams navigate the tradeoffs inherent in AI-assisted development at scale, ensuring consistent practices across the fellowship.
Success Measures
Success for Staff Engineers is measured by their impact as force multipliers across the fellowship. Key indicators include:

Delivery: Successful delivery of complex cross-team initiatives
Technical Quality: Measurable improvements in fellowship-wide technical quality or velocity
Bench Growth: Development of engineers who advance their capabilities, so that the organizational bench grows in areas they lead. Teams they work with become more technically self-sufficient over time, not more dependent on the Staff Engineer.
Pattern Adoption: Adoption of their technical patterns and standards across teams
Blocker Resolution: Effective resolution of organizational and technical blockers
Strategic Positioning: Strategic technical contributions that position the fellowship for future success

Success measures vary by operating mode. Tech Lead-oriented Staff Engineers demonstrate success through team velocity improvements, engineer growth, and cross-team coordination. Architect-oriented Staff Engineers demonstrate success through adopted standards, architectural coherence, and long-term platform stability. Solver-oriented Staff Engineers demonstrate success through resolution of critical cross-cutting problems and reusable solutions.

Staff Engineers are evaluated not just on their personal output but on how they elevate the entire fellowship's technical capability and execution.
Role Boundaries
P4-1s are not:
P3-IIs who work on harder problems - The shift is team to fellowship and personal output to force multiplication. The concrete line against P3-II is scope: they own platforms or multiple systems across teams within the fellowship, and their patterns are adopted fellowship-wide, not just team-wide.
P3-IIs who haven't been promoted yet - P4-II requires all three of: consistent cross-fellowship impact, ownership of the technical direction of cross-fellowship initiatives, and patterns adopted department-wide. P4-I's influence is fellowship-scoped: they contribute expertise to cross-fellowship work when asked rather than owning its direction, mentor P3-IIs toward Staff rather than sponsoring P4-I candidates, and operate primarily in one archetype. Missing any of the three keeps the placement at P4-I.
Engineering Managers without reports - They own technical direction, not team delivery, performance management, or resource allocation. Those accountabilities remain with EMs.
Architects disconnected from the code - They keep a hands-on connection to the codebase - roughly 30-40% of their time on technical work including reviews, prototyping, and spikes - and understand the practical realities of implementation.

Advancement to P4-II requires demonstrating consistent impact across multiple fellowships AND organizational need for this level of technical leadership. Representing approximately 3% of engineers, Staff Engineer IIs shape how engineering operates across their domain, establishing patterns and standards that become department-wide practices.
Operating Modes (Archetypes)
Staff Engineer IIs blend the operating modes described under Staff Engineer (P4-I) rather than working primarily in one, shifting emphasis as organizational priorities move. Accountability & Decision Rights
Staff Engineer IIs are Responsible for technical direction spanning multiple fellowships, while Directors and Senior Directors are Accountable for organizational outcomes within their domains. They are frequently the designated technical owner on initiatives where no single fellowship holds authority, which makes them the tie-breaker on technical questions that would otherwise stall between Directors.

Because they set direction that other Staff Engineers follow, Staff Engineer IIs are Responsible for technical work performed largely by people who do not report to them and who do not report to a common manager. This operates through influence and earned trust. When alignment cannot be reached, escalation runs to the Directors accountable for the affected fellowships, not to the Staff Engineer II imposing a decision.

Staff Engineer IIs own technical strategy for major domains spanning multiple fellowships. [P5] Principal Engineer, Career
Domain expert, mentor, technical leader defining and driving technical strategy
Role Overview
Principal Engineers are Weave's foremost technical experts and highest individual contributors, recognized both internally and externally for their deep or broad expertise. Reaching P5 is even more difficult than advancing to P4 and is generally not anticipated for most engineers' careers—representing truly exceptional technical leadership at company scale. Representing approximately 1-2% of engineers, Principal Engineers require both demonstrated company-wide strategic impact AND organizational need for this level of technical leadership. They operate as functional leads across multiple fellowships and departments, shaping technical strategy, mentoring broadly, driving company-critical initiatives, and solving root problems with long-term vision. Their impeccable character and embodiment of Weave's values make them highly visible and respected leaders whose influence shapes the company's technical trajectory.
Operating Modes (Archetypes)
Principal Engineers typically blend multiple operating modes, with the flexibility to shift emphasis based on company needs:

Architect Mode: Owns technical direction and quality for major domains spanning multiple fellowships or departments. Sets multi-year architectural vision and ensures technical decisions position Weave for long-term success. Leads "constellations of teams" through influence rather than authority.

Solver Mode: Tackles the most complex, ambiguous company-level problems that others cannot resolve. Works across organizational boundaries to address root causes rather than symptoms. May focus deeply on one critical area or move between strategic hotspots as needs shift.

Right Hand Mode: Extends executive attention by operating with borrowed scope and authority. Works at the intersection of business, technology, people, and process to help executives navigate complex organizational challenges. Requires deep alignment with leadership's approach and values.

Most Principal Engineers operate primarily in Architect or Solver mode, blending elements based on organizational context. The Right Hand mode is less common and emerges based on specific executive partnership needs.
Accountability & Decision Rights
Principal Engineers are accountable for technology at company scale—technical strategy, architectural direction, and engineering excellence across departments. They are not accountable for organizational outcomes, team delivery, or resource allocation. This accountability distinction is what separates Principal Engineers from Directors and Senior Directors, not technical capability.

Principal Engineers own: Company-wide technical strategy, cross-department architectural decisions, engineering standards and practices, technical vision spanning multiple years, and resolution of the most complex technical challenges.

Directors and Senior Directors own: Organizational outcomes, resource allocation across teams, headcount and budget decisions, delivery commitments, performance management, and organizational health.

When Directors are highly technical (as Weave's are), the distinction is not capability but primary accountability. Both may participate deeply in technical decisions, but the Principal Engineer is the designated owner of technical direction while the Director is the designated owner of delivery and organizational outcomes.
Technical Scope & Ownership
Principal Engineers own technical strategy and direction for major domains that span multiple fellowships or departments. They influence architectural direction across the department, ensuring technical decisions position Weave for multi-year success. Rather than focusing on individual system ownership, they shape how entire domains evolve, understanding complex cross-system interactions and ensuring long-term platform stability and scalability. They identify and solve core company-level technical problems, working across domains and departments to address root causes rather than symptoms. They anticipate technical challenges and opportunities well into the future, guiding the company with their extensive experience and strategic vision.

Principal Engineers typically dedicate approximately 20-30% of their time to hands-on technical work, with the remainder focused on strategic leadership, cross-department coordination, and organizational influence. Their hands-on contributions are highly selective and strategic—architectural reviews, technical due diligence for major initiatives, proof-of-concepts for critical technical decisions, and mentorship through code. The reduced coding time reflects the higher leverage of their strategic impact at company scale.
Autonomy & Problem-Solving
Principal Engineers proactively identify company-level technical problems and opportunities that significantly impact Weave's success, operating with the highest degree of autonomy to shape what the company should solve and set strategic technical direction. They make difficult decisions based on desired outcomes for the company, not specific tactical outputs. They excel in the most ambiguous situations where both problems and solutions are unclear, charting paths forward through complex technical and organizational landscapes. They lead creation of technical documentation and communication for the most strategic, highest-impact initiatives, setting direction that influences multiple fellowships and departments. They align multiple teams and projects across organizational boundaries, ensuring coherent execution of complex, multi-quarter initiatives.
Relationship with Directors
Principal Engineers and Directors are strategic partners with complementary accountabilities. Principal Engineers report to Directors, but the relationship is designed to work as a partnership rather than a chain of command. Directors set the context and the commitments. Principal Engineers set the technical path that makes those commitments achievable.
Principal Engineers lead architectural evolution. They author technical plans, define architectural roadmaps, and surface the dependencies and scope that determine whether a complex initiative succeeds or stalls. They are thought partners to Directors, Senior Directors, and executive leadership, and their judgment shapes strategic decisions well beyond their own teams.
Directors own timely delivery of business outcomes. They collate data across fellowships, prioritize initiatives, allocate engineering resources, and make the tradeoff calls that keep work on track. They build the conditions in which teams do their best work and carry accountability for results landing when the business needs them.
Both roles bring deep technical expertise. What separates them is what they are accountable for. When a technical decision carries organizational weight such as headcount, timeline, or team structure, both engage, each leading from their own vantage point. Where the two pull against each other, they resolve it together, and the Director carries final accountability for the delivery commitment.
At its best, this partnership produces something neither role achieves alone: architecture built to last, delivered on the timeline the business is counting on.
Collaboration, Mentorship & Influence
Principal Engineers drive company-wide collaboration, lead out in shaping engineering culture, working across all levels of the organization to align technical strategy with business objectives. They actively mentor and elevate technical capabilities across the department, developing engineers at all levels (P1-P4) and creating lasting impact through their mentees' growth. They are instrumental in setting department-wide engineering standards and practices, with their approaches becoming templates for how engineering operates at Weave. Their words and actions significantly influence those around them, requiring an exceptional blend of technical and people skills.
Product Partnership & Documentation
Principal Engineers maintain strategic partnerships with executive-level Product and business leadership, translating high-level business vision into multi-year technical strategies. They provide critical technical perspective on company strategic roadmap decisions, helping executives understand technical possibilities, constraints, and opportunities. They lead or provide architectural oversight for technical documentation on the most strategic initiatives, ensuring technical approaches enable rather than constrain business ambitions. They work across multiple Product leaders, helping align product strategy with technical reality and capability.
Technical Leadership & Strategic Influence
Principal Engineers shape and drive company-wide technical improvements, setting standards for engineering excellence and innovation across all departments. They establish technical vision that becomes multi-quarter or multi-year company strategy, positioning Weave ahead of competitors. They establish architectural patterns and guardrails that enable safe, effective AI tool adoption across the department while managing risks related to code quality, security, and intellectual property. Principal Engineers drive transformational improvements in engineering productivity through strategic AI adoption, measuring and communicating impact at the organizational level. [M2] Team Lead
A senior engineer with formal responsibility for team operations
Role Overview
Team Leads are accountable for the consistent delivery of valuable, high-quality software from their small team. They possess end-to-end ownership of their team's engineering outcomes, encompassing code quality, system health, technical decisions, and the direct impact of their team's work on customers. Team Leads balance significant hands-on technical work (approximately 80% of their time), including taking tickets and writing code, with foundational people leadership (approximately 20%), serving as a developmental role for engineers transitioning toward Engineering Manager positions. They are responsible for guiding and developing their small team, ensuring alignment with fellowship (the group of teams managed by a Director) and departmental objectives, and maintaining the stability of their team's services.
Team Structure & Leadership
Team Leads lead small teams of 3-5 engineers, typically consisting of P2 and P3 level engineers, often collaborating with P4 senior engineers. They report to an Engineering Manager, a Senior Engineering Manager or an Engineering Director. Team Leads are accountable for the growth and development of their team members, including performance management, providing continuous feedback, and supporting career development. While their people management load is lighter than that of an Engineering Manager, Team Leads gain critical leadership experience in building team culture, addressing performance issues, and developing engineering talent—essential preparation for future EM responsibilities.
Product Partnership & Technical Work
Team Leads work directly with Product Managers to co-manage the roadmap for their team. They are responsible for translating product vision into executable engineering work, ensuring work is dev-ready before implementation begins, and maintaining continuous communication with the PM and stakeholders throughout the development process. This partnership requires Team Leads to make informed tradeoffs that balance functionality with technical sustainability, push back on projects that pose significant risks, and ensure those risks are either mitigated or clearly communicated and documented. Team Leads ensure their team builds the right solutions efficiently, keeping projects focused on critical objectives.
Strategic Planning & Execution
Team Leads make key decisions regarding technical architecture, priority and scope, and task assignments—particularly when their team cannot reach consensus. They are responsible for planning work, assigning tasks aligned with each team member's skills and growth areas, and reporting project status to leadership. Appendix: Key Definitions
Architectural Patterns
Reusable solutions to common software design problems. These include established approaches to structuring code, organizing systems, and solving technical challenges that have been proven effective within the organization. Junior engineers follow these patterns; senior engineers propose and define them.
Career Track
Role levels (P3-P5 for ICs, M3-M6 for managers) where continued progression is not required for successful performance. Engineers and managers at these levels have achieved professional mastery and can maintain highly successful careers without advancing further. Promotions from Career Track roles require both demonstrated performance at the next level AND organizational need.
Cardinality
The expected ratio or distribution of a role within the engineering organization. For example, "approximately 10% of engineers" for Staff Engineers (P4) indicates this is a selective level where roughly 10% of engineers operate, reflecting both the high bar for performance and organizational need for this level of technical leadership.
Code Quality Accountability
Senior engineers and above are accountable for ensuring code quality standards are maintained before code is merged. This accountability means they review code with sufficient expertise to identify issues, coach engineers on quality practices, and make informed judgments about whether code meets production standards. The goal is to maintain high standards while teaching others to meet those standards independently, creating a culture where quality is everyone's responsibility but senior engineers ensure consistency.
Cross-Fellowship Collaboration
Working effectively across multiple fellowships (groups of teams managed by different Directors) to solve problems, share knowledge, or deliver initiatives that span organizational boundaries. This requires navigating different team cultures, priorities, and technical approaches.
Cross-Functional Work
Collaboration between engineering and other disciplines such as Product Management, Design, Operations, or Sales. This requires understanding different perspectives, communicating effectively across disciplines, and aligning technical work with broader business objectives.
Department
The entire Product and Engineering organization at Weave. When a Principal Engineer (P5) has "department-wide" impact, they're influencing technical direction, standards, or strategy across all of engineering.
Domain
A major area of technical ownership that typically spans multiple systems or platforms. Examples might include "payments," "communications," "analytics," or "platform infrastructure." Principal Engineers (P5) typically own technical strategy for entire domains.
Fellowship
The group of teams (typically 3+ teams with their Engineering Managers) managed by an Engineering Director, usually organized around a related technical domain. A fellowship represents a significant organizational scope larger than a single team but smaller than the entire department.
Force Multiplier
An engineer whose impact extends beyond their direct work by enabling others to be more productive. This is achieved through mentorship, creating reusable solutions, establishing standards, removing blockers, or providing architectural guidance. Staff and Principal Engineers are expected to be force multipliers.
Growth Track
Role levels (P1-P2 for ICs, M2 for managers) where progression to the next level is expected as part of normal career development. Engineers and managers in Growth Track roles are developing toward professional competence, and lack of progression may indicate performance concerns. Promotions from Growth Track do not require organizational need—if performance is demonstrated, promotion is expected.
Incident Commander
A rotating operational responsibility at Director level (M5) where the on-call Director leads and coordinates response to production incidents. The Incident Commander receives incident alerts, makes critical decisions about incident response strategy (including rollback decisions), coordinates resources across teams, facilitates communication with stakeholders, creates incident documentation, and ensures thorough post-incident analysis. This role requires rapid decision-making under pressure, deep technical understanding, and strong organizational coordination skills. Following incidents, the Incident Commander ensures postmortems are held, remediation work is tracked, and affected teams prioritize preventive actions. Senior Directors (M6) may participate in rotation or provide escalation support during critical incidents.
Individual Contributor (IC)
An engineer who creates impact through technical work and influence rather than through managing people. ICs progress from P1 (Jr Engineer) through P5 (Principal Engineer), with scope and influence expanding at each level while remaining in individual contributor roles.
Mentorship
The practice of actively developing less experienced engineers through regular guidance, code reviews, pair programming, answering questions, and sharing knowledge. Expectations for mentorship scale by level: P2s help P1s occasionally, P3s actively mentor P1-P2s, P4s elevate P1-P3s across the fellowship, and P5s develop engineers at all levels across the department.
Organizational Need
A requirement for Career Track promotions (P3+ for ICs, M3+ for managers) that goes beyond individual performance. Even if an engineer or manager is performing at the next level, promotion requires that the organization needs another position at that level in that area. This ensures appropriate balance of levels and prevents title inflation. For example, a fellowship might have several engineers performing at P4 level but only need/support 1-2 P4 positions.
Platform
A collection of interconnected systems, services, and tools that work together to provide capabilities used by multiple teams or products. Staff Engineers (P4) typically own platforms that span teams, ensuring architectural coherence and strategic evolution across multiple systems.
Problem Definition (What vs. How)
The distinction between what needs to be solved and how to solve it:

What: The problem, objective, or outcome to achieve
How: The implementation approach, technical decisions, and specific solution

Junior engineers are given both "what" and "how." As engineers advance, they take on more responsibility for defining the "how" (P2), then the "what" (P3), and eventually proactively discovering important problems to solve (P4-P5).
Strategic vs. Tactical
Tactical: Short-term, execution-focused decisions about specific tasks or immediate problems
Strategic: Long-term, vision-oriented thinking about future needs, systemic improvements, and positioning for success

Junior engineers operate tactically; senior engineers balance tactical execution with strategic thinking; Staff and Principal Engineers operate primarily strategically, shaping direction for multiple teams or the entire organization.
System
A cohesive collection of services, databases, and infrastructure that works together to deliver specific functionality. A system has clear boundaries, owns particular data or business logic, and can be operated and maintained independently. Senior Engineers (P3) typically own one or more complete systems end-to-end.
System Ownership
Complete accountability for a system's success, including its reliability, performance, security, maintainability, and evolution. System ownership includes operational responsibilities (on-call, incidents), technical decisions (architecture, tech stack), and long-term stewardship (managing technical debt, planning evolution). This level of ownership begins at P3 (Senior Engineer).
T-Shaped Engineer
An engineer with deep expertise in one or more specific areas (the vertical bar of the "T") combined with broad competence across many areas (the horizontal bar). Staff and Principal Engineers are expected to be T-shaped, enabling them to solve specialized problems while also contributing across diverse technical domains.
Technical Debt
Code, architecture, or infrastructure that is suboptimal or outdated but remains in production because it was the right tradeoff at the time. Technical debt requires eventual repayment through refactoring or replacement. Senior engineers (P3+) are expected to actively manage technical debt, balancing feature delivery with system sustainability.
Technical Project Document (TPD)
A structured document that defines the problem to be solved, evaluates solution options, describes the chosen approach, and outlines implementation plans for significant engineering projects. P3+ engineers lead creation of TPDs, with Staff and Principal Engineers leading TPDs for initiatives spanning multiple teams or fellowships. See TPD template for full structure.
Technical Strategy
The long-term plan for how technology will evolve to support business objectives. This includes architectural direction, technology choices, platform investments, and technical capability building. Staff Engineers shape technical strategy for their fellowship; Principal Engineers shape strategy at department or company level.


Role-Specific Term Clarifications
"As Needed" (P5 Cardinality)
Principal Engineers are hired or promoted based on organizational need for their specific expertise or strategic leadership in particular domains. The "1-2%" or "as needed" designation indicates these roles are not evenly distributed but rather created when the organization needs deep subject matter expertise or strategic technical leadership that doesn't currently exist.
Reporting Structure Flexibility
Staff Engineers (P4) may report to Engineering Managers, Senior Engineering Managers, Engineering Directors, or Senior Engineering Directors depending on organizational structure and the nature of their work. This flexibility reflects that Staff Engineers work across teams and need strong relationships with engineering leadership at various levels. Principal Engineers (P5) typically report to Directors or Senior Directors given their strategic, cross-fellowship scope.
